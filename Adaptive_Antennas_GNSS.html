00Adaptive Antennas for GNSS
An engineer's introduction

Adaptive Antennas for GNSS

A GNSS signal reaches the ground about 20 dB below the thermal noise, so a 10 mW jammer a few kilometres away can blind a receiver. An adaptive antenna is a small array of antenna elements whose outputs are weighted and summed so that the combined pattern puts deep nulls toward the interference while still hearing the satellites. This presentation builds that idea from waves and phase, through two antennas, to the algorithms, the hardware and the industry.

01Introduction

Why GNSS needs adaptive antennas

A GPS satellite is about 20 000 km away and transmits roughly the power of a few car headlights. By the time the signal reaches you it is weaker than the background hiss of the receiver itself; the receiver only finds it by correlating with a known code for a long time. A jammer on the ground, even a cheap one, is a billion times stronger. A normal antenna hears every direction about equally, so it hears the jammer too. An adaptive antenna chooses what to hear.

An ordinary GNSS antenna is a fixed reception pattern antenna (FRPA): one element, a pattern that covers the whole sky and is fixed at the factory. A controlled reception pattern antenna (CRPA) has several elements, typically 2 to 7 for GNSS (up to 16 in some designs), and electronics that combine them with complex weights. The weights are recomputed thousands of times per second from the received data, so the pattern adapts to whatever interference is present.

    A radio wave is a moving pattern of phase

    A plane wave from a satellite crosses two antennas
    The straight lines are wavefronts (crests), one wavelength apart. The wave reaches the right antenna earlier than the left one by the extra path . The two phasors (section 04) show the phase each antenna sees at this instant.
    Think of ocean swell rolling onto a beach at an angle: the crests hit one end of a pier before the other. A satellite is so far away that its wavefronts are flat (a plane wave) when they reach us. Two antennas a few centimetres apart see the same wave, just slightly shifted in time. That tiny shift, a fraction of a nanosecond, is all an array needs to tell directions apart.

    03Foundations

    One antenna: a gain pattern and a polarisation

    An antenna is a funnel for radio energy. It does not create power; it only favours some directions over others. Its gain pattern says how much it favours each direction compared with an ideal antenna that hears all directions equally (an isotropic antenna, 0 dBi). A GNSS antenna is built to like the sky and dislike the ground.
    Gain in direction (θ, φ), relative to isotropic
    A simple model of a patch over a ground plane (θ = angle from zenith)

    Three properties matter for GNSS:

    • Hemispherical coverage. Satellites can be anywhere above the horizon, so the antenna has a broad beam, typically +3 to +5 dBic at zenith falling to about −5 dBic at 5–10° elevation.
    • Right-hand circular polarisation (RHCP). GNSS satellites transmit RHCP. A reflection off the ground or a wall mostly flips it to left-hand (LHCP), so an antenna with good axial ratio rejects much of the multipath.
    • Low gain toward the ground (good front-to-back ratio), which helps against reflections and against jammers below the horizon.

    The catch: ground-based jammers sit near the horizon, exactly where the antenna still has to hear low satellites. A fixed pattern cannot separate them. Several elements can (section 05 onwards).

    The units dBic mean gain relative to an isotropic, circularly polarised antenna. The pattern model above is qualitative: real patterns come from measurement in an anechoic chamber and depend on the ground plane size.

    04Foundations

    Phasors: adding two waves is adding two arrows

    Two waves of the same frequency always add up to another wave of that frequency. Whether the result is big or small depends only on how their crests line up. Draw each wave as an arrow (a phasor): its length is the amplitude, its angle is the phase. Adding the waves is just putting the arrows head to tail. Aligned arrows add up; opposite arrows cancel. Every beam and every null in this presentation is this picture.
    A carrier as the real part of a rotating complex number
    Sum of two waves
    Equal amplitudes, phase difference Δφ

    In an array, each element multiplies its signal by a complex weight : a gain and a phase shift. The weights are the only thing the adaptive processor controls.

    Note the null needs both conditions: exactly opposite phase and equal amplitude. With 1% amplitude error the best cancellation is dB; this sets the depth of real nulls (section 22).

    05Two antennas

    Two antennas plus a phase shifter make a steerable pattern

    Connect two antennas to one receiver through a phase shifter on the second one. For each arrival direction, the wave reaches the two antennas with a different phase (section 02). The phase shifter adds its own fixed phase. Where the two phases cancel, the outputs add up: a beam. Where they differ by 180°, the outputs cancel: a null. Turn the phase-shifter knob and the whole pattern swings around.

    06Two antennas

    Two antennas in 3D: one null cone, aimed at a jammer

    The two-antenna pattern of section 05 is really three-dimensional: it is the same in every plane that contains the baseline, rotated around it. So the null is a cone around the baseline axis, and where that cone meets the sky it draws a curve, not a point. Aim the curve through the jammer and the jammer disappears; any satellite that happens to lie on the same curve disappears too. This is the simplest adaptive antenna, used in low-cost two-element anti-jam products for cars and drones.

    07Arrays

    More antennas: narrower beams and more nulls

    Put N antennas in a row, evenly spaced. Each new element adds one more arrow to the sum. Straight ahead all arrows line up, so the beam gets N times stronger; a little off to the side the arrows fan out into a circle and add to zero. The more arrows, the sooner they close the circle, so the beam narrows like 1/N and the pattern gets N − 1 nulls.

    08Arrays

    The weights are the whole story: drag them and watch the pattern

    Four elements, four complex weights, one pattern
    Left: each weight is a draggable arrow inside the unit circle (length = gain, angle = phase). Right: the polar pattern for a 4-element line at λ/2 (upper half = sky). Red marks are the targeted null directions in the presets.
    A beam former has only one set of knobs: one gain and one phase per element. Steering a beam means choosing phases that cancel the wave's phase progression in one direction. Placing a null means choosing weights whose arrows sum to zero for another direction. An adaptive antenna is just a machine that turns these knobs automatically from the data.

    Beam toward θ₀ (phase-only, "delay and sum")
    Null toward θJ: a linear constraint
    Beam at θ₀ and nulls at θ1 … θK (K ≤ N − 1): project out the jammers

    The last formula is the "deterministic null steering" solution: it needs to know the jammer directions. GNSS adaptive antennas usually do not know them, so they estimate the interference from the data instead (sections 12–13). The result looks the same: weights whose pattern has holes where the interference is.

    Note how small changes in a weight move a null a lot, but barely change the main beam: nulls are sensitive, beams are robust. That is why nulls need precise, calibrated channels (section 22).

    09Arrays

    Planar arrays: the controlled reception pattern antenna

    A line of antennas can only tell angles along its own axis; its patterns are cones around the line (section 06). Put the elements in a plane, and the array can distinguish azimuth and elevation. GNSS CRPAs are small flat discs: four elements in a square or seven in a hexagon (one in the centre, six around it), spaced about λ/2, under one radome.

    10Interference

    What the array is up against: kinds of interference

    Anything that puts power into the GNSS band raises the noise the receiver must fight. Some of it is accidental (a faulty TV amplifier, harmonics from a nearby transmitter, radar or DME pulses), some is deliberate (cheap in-car "privacy" jammers, military jammers, spoofers that imitate the satellites). An adaptive antenna does not care what the signal is, only where it comes from: anything strong from a direction gets a null.
    TypeWhat it looks likeTypical source
    CW / narrowbandOne tone or a few kHz wideHarmonics, oscillators, simple jammers
    Swept (chirp)A tone sweeping across the band every few µsPersonal privacy devices (PPDs)
    Wideband noiseFlat noise over the whole signal bandMilitary and high-end jammers
    PulsedStrong short pulses, low duty cycleDME/TACAN near L5/E5, radars near L2/E6
    Matched spectrumLooks like a GNSS signal (sinc²)Spoofers, sophisticated jammers

    Receivers already have single-antenna defences: notch filters remove CW lines, pulse blanking removes pulses, and an adaptive quantiser keeps the ADC from saturating. These work in the frequency or time domain. They fail against wideband noise and against matched-spectrum signals, because there is nothing to separate in frequency or time. Only space (direction) and polarisation still separate them. That is the niche of adaptive antennas.

    11Interference

    The jamming budget: how many decibels decide whether you track

    Effective C/N₀ versus jammer distance
    Grey: single antenna. Blue: the same receiver behind an adaptive array that removes the chosen number of decibels from the jammer. Where a curve drops below the dashed threshold the receiver loses lock; that distance is the denial radius.
    Jamming is a power contest after correlation. The receiver's despreading turns the satellite back into a strong narrow signal and spreads the jammer out. Whatever jammer power is left acts like extra noise. Every decibel the array removes from the jammer shrinks the jammed area: 20 dB of suppression cuts the denial radius by 10×, 40 dB by 100×, because free-space loss grows by 20 dB per decade of distance.

    12Adaptive algorithms

    The covariance matrix: how the array "sees" the interference

    The adaptive processor never looks at a map of jammers. It only sees N streams of samples. Multiply every stream by the conjugate of every other one and average: you get an N × N table of correlations, the covariance matrix. Noise is uncorrelated between elements, so it only fills the diagonal. A jammer is the same waveform at every element, just phase-shifted, so it fills the whole matrix with a pattern that encodes its direction. The GNSS signals are far below the noise, so they hardly show up at all, which is exactly what makes the trick work.
    Snapshot model: satellites + jammers + noise
    Covariance (satellite terms are ~20 dB below σ² and can be dropped)
    Estimated from K snapshots
    Eigen-decomposition

    Here is the jammer-to-noise ratio (JNR) per element. The large eigenvalues count the jammers and their eigenvectors span the "interference subspace". Every adaptive algorithm that follows is a way of using or to suppress that subspace.

    Note the factor N: the array collects the jammer coherently, so it stands N times (10 log N dB) above the per-element JNR in the eigenvalues. Two jammers close together in angle share almost the same eigenvector, so one eigenvalue grows and the other shrinks: that is the resolution limit of a small array.

    13Adaptive algorithms

    Power inversion: minimise everything, keep one element

    The classic GNSS anti-jam algorithm needs no knowledge of where the satellites or jammers are. Its rule is: make the total output power as small as possible, but keep the weight of one reference element fixed at 1 so the trivial answer "all weights zero" is forbidden. Since the GNSS signals are buried below the noise, the output power is almost all jammer plus noise. Minimising it digs nulls toward everything strong and leaves the rest of the sky roughly like a single element. The result is a pattern that is roughly the inverse of the received power distribution, hence the name.

    The last line is remarkable: in an ideal array the residual gets smaller as the jammer gets stronger, because a strong jammer is easier to "see" in . For N = 4 and ξ = 50 dB the output JNR is about −61 dB (section 21 works this out). Real nulls stop at 30–50 dB because of channel mismatch and bandwidth (sections 17, 22).

    Pros: simple, robust, needs no attitude or satellite directions, produces one RF output that any existing receiver can use. Cons: no beam gain toward satellites, a satellite near a jammer is lost with it, and the pattern (hence the carrier phase) toward each satellite changes as the weights adapt (section 18).

    14Adaptive algorithms

    MVDR: null the jammers and point a beam at each satellite

    Power inversion versus MVDR for the same jammers
    Left: power inversion (one pattern for all satellites). Right: MVDR for the selected satellite (violet ring). Drag the jammers on either map. Steering error points the MVDR constraint off the true satellite, as a wrong attitude estimate would: the beam misses and the satellite's gain falls.
    If the receiver knows where each satellite is relative to the antenna (from the ephemeris, its own position and the platform's attitude), it can do better than power inversion. For each satellite it asks the array: minimise output power, but keep unit gain in the satellite's direction. The result is a pattern with nulls on the jammers and a beam on that satellite. Doing this for every tracked satellite, in parallel, is called beam-per-satellite or digital beamforming.
    Minimum variance distortionless response (Capon, 1969)
    Output SINR (optimal for a known as)
    Power inversion is the special case as → e1

    Gains over power inversion: up to dB toward each satellite (8.5 dB for 7 elements), a distortionless response (known gain and phase toward the satellite, which removes the carrier-phase bias of section 18), and better performance when a jammer is close to a satellite.

    Costs: one beamformer per satellite (so the receiver itself must be multi-channel and digital), an attitude source (IMU) and an accurate calibration of the real steering vectors, including the platform's effect on them. A mismatch between the assumed and true points the beam in the wrong place (the slider on the left). For a signal above the noise it is worse: MVDR then treats the real signal as interference and nulls it ("signal cancellation"). GNSS signals are below the noise, so this mainly matters for strong pseudolites or when beamforming toward a spoofer.

    15Adaptive algorithms

    Adapting in real time: estimate the covariance, or follow the gradient

    The formulas need , but the receiver only has samples. There are two classic ways forward. Sample matrix inversion (SMI) averages a block of K snapshots into and inverts it, which is fast and precise once K is a few times N. Gradient (LMS-type) loops nudge the weights a little after every sample in the direction that lowers the output power, which is cheap in hardware but slower, especially when jammer powers differ a lot.

    16Adaptive algorithms

    Degrees of freedom: N elements, N − 1 nulls

    Residual jammer power versus number of jammers (Monte Carlo)
    Each dot is one random geometry; the lines are medians. Up to N − 1 jammers the residual stays far below the noise (0 dB). One jammer more and it jumps to roughly the jammer power minus the array gain: there is no weight vector left that can null them all.
    Each null is a linear equation , and an N-element array has N complex weights, one of which is spent on keeping the output from being zero. So it can satisfy N − 1 null equations at most. Two jammers in almost the same direction behave like one; a jammer that is spread out (by multipath from nearby structures, or wideband across a big array) uses up more than one degree of freedom.
    Null conditions as a linear system
    Non-zero solution exists only if
    • Too many jammers: the algorithm still minimises power, so it puts shallow nulls "in between" and the output JNR jumps up (figure).
    • Jammer near a satellite: the null has a finite width (about the beamwidth of section 07, 40–60° for a small array), so satellites within roughly 10–20° of a jammer lose a lot of gain.
    • Low-elevation geometry: ground jammers all sit near the horizon. For a flat array the horizon is where its angular resolution is weakest in elevation, so many jammers crowd the same region.
    • Remedies: more elements, larger spacing (within the grating-lobe limit), time taps (STAP adds degrees of freedom for wideband and multipath, section 17), and polarisation-diverse elements.
    17Wideband processing

    When one weight per element is not enough: STAP and SFAP

    A single complex weight is a perfect phase shift at one frequency. A wideband jammer occupies 20 MHz or more, and at the band edges the geometry's delay looks like a slightly different phase. Worse, the RF channels behind each element are never identical: their filters have slightly different delays and ripples across the band. A null that is perfect at the centre frequency fills in at the edges. The fix is to give each element a short filter instead of a single weight, so the weights can vary with frequency: space-time adaptive processing (STAP), or its FFT-domain twin, space-frequency (SFAP).
    Element n response at baseband frequency f (geometry delay τn, channel mismatch δn, εn)
    STAP: M taps per element, spacing Ts
    Wideband covariance (flat jammer over bandwidth B) and power inversion on NM weights
    Rule of thumb: the narrowband null is limited by the phase spread across the band

    Quick check for a two-channel canceller: λ/2 spacing at L1 gives at most ns of geometry delay; over B = 20 MHz that limits the null to about −39 dB. A 2 ns channel delay mismatch over 20 MHz limits it to about −22 dB. So for small GNSS arrays the channel mismatch dominates. A larger array can spend spare degrees of freedom on it, but not when every degree of freedom is already used for a jammer; a few taps (M = 3–7) fix most of it.

    SFAP does the same per FFT bin: transform each channel, compute one weight vector per bin, and transform back. It is efficient for many taps.

    The price of a null: biases in carrier phase and code delay

    Carrier-phase bias toward each direction caused by the adaptive weights
    Colour = , the phase change compared with the reference element alone, in degrees (blue positive, orange negative). Drag the jammer: every satellite's phase shifts by a different amount, and the shift changes whenever the jammer moves. Chips convert it to millimetres at L1 (360° = 190 mm).
    Precise GNSS (RTK, PPP, attitude, timing) measures the carrier phase to millimetres and the code delay to centimetres. An adaptive antenna changes the effective antenna for every satellite: the combined pattern has a gain and a phase toward each direction, and both change as the weights adapt. With STAP, the filters also add a group delay that can differ from satellite to satellite. To the receiver this looks like a moving antenna phase centre.

    In the worked example (section 21), the satellite far from the jammer shifts by only 0.25°, but a satellite about 24° from the jammer shifts by 59°: 31 mm at L1, many times an RTK error budget. It differs per satellite, so it does not cancel in the clock estimate, and it changes whenever the jammer or the platform moves.

    Mitigations in use:

    • Distortionless constraints (MVDR, section 14): gain 1 and phase 0 toward each satellite by construction.
    • Compensation: the receiver knows w, so it can compute and from a calibrated array model and correct the measurements.
    • Constrained STAP: symmetric (linear-phase) tap constraints keep the group delay equal across directions.
    • Calibration of each channel's delay and phase, and of the array installed on the platform.
    19Beyond jamming

    Spoofing: all the fake satellites come from one direction

    A spoofer transmits counterfeit GNSS signals, so the receiver computes a false position or time. The signals can be perfect copies in shape and power, so frequency and time tricks do not reveal them. But a single spoofer is one transmitter: all of its fake satellites arrive from the same direction. Two antennas measure the phase difference of each satellite. Real satellites are spread across the sky, so their phase differences are all different; spoofed ones are all the same. That is a very hard signature to fake.
    Carrier-phase single difference between two elements (baseline b)
    Spoofed: one direction usp for every PRN
    Simple test statistic: how aligned the single differences are

    Integer ambiguities and the unknown hardware phase do not matter for this test, because it only checks whether the phases are all alike. With more elements the receiver can estimate each signal's direction and compare it with the direction predicted from the ephemeris, then null the spoofer like a jammer.

    Limits: several spoofers in different places, a spoofer on the platform itself, or a spoofer that knows the array geometry and orientation (practically very hard). A single antenna that moves (a phone in a hand) can do a weaker version of this test as a synthetic array (section 25).

    The same array can point at the jammer: MUSIC direction finding

    Azimuth spectrum of two jammers (7-element array, 5° elevation)
    Violet: MUSIC pseudo-spectrum (sharp peaks). Grey: conventional beam scan (Bartlett), limited by the array's wide beam. Orange ticks: true azimuths. Bring the jammers within about 30° of each other: the Bartlett scan merges them while MUSIC still separates them at good JNR.
    The covariance matrix knows the jammer directions (section 12). The noise eigenvectors are, by construction, orthogonal to every jammer's steering vector. So scan all directions and ask: how close is this direction's steering vector to being orthogonal to the noise subspace? Where it is (almost) exactly orthogonal, there is a jammer. That is MUSIC (multiple signal classification, Schmidt). Arrays used this way support interference detection and localisation: several receivers measuring bearings can triangulate a jammer.

    22Engineering

    Real hardware: why nulls stop at 30–50 dB

    The mathematics promises −110 dB nulls; real systems deliver roughly 25–50 dB per jammer. A null is a subtraction of two almost equal numbers, so anything that makes the channels differently wrong limits it: gain and phase mismatch between RF chains, filters with different ripples, mutual coupling between nearby elements, the platform's own reflections, temperature drift, the ADC's dynamic range. Calibration and STAP recover some of it; physics sets the rest.
    Two-channel cancellation with amplitude error ε and phase error δ
    Example: 0.1 dB and 1°
    ADC dynamic range for b bits (sine, full scale)
    • ADC and AGC. The jammer must be digitised without clipping before it is nulled. With 50–60 dB JNR the converter needs roughly 10–14 bits; a classic 1–2 bit GNSS front end saturates and the array can no longer help.
    • Mutual coupling. At λ/2 spacing each element picks up some of its neighbours' currents. The true steering vectors differ from the textbook , which hurts MVDR and direction finding more than power inversion.
    • Size. λ/2 at L1 is 9.5 cm, at L5 12.7 cm. A 7-element L1/L5 array is therefore around 25–35 cm across. Shrinking it (high-permittivity substrates, sub-λ/2 spacing) costs bandwidth, efficiency and null resolution.
    • Installation. The airframe or vehicle roof changes every element's pattern differently, so arrays are often calibrated as installed, or modelled with electromagnetic simulation.

    The usable range runs from the quantisation floor to full scale, with about 10 dB of headroom for the jammer's peaks. If the noise falls below the quantisation floor, the information the array needs to see the satellites is gone.
    23History & industry

    History: radar invented adaptive arrays, GNSS made them small

    Adaptive arrays were born in radar in the late 1950s and 1960s, to stop jammers coming in through the sidelobes. The theory (LMS, Applebaum's loop, Capon's MVDR, sample-matrix inversion, MUSIC) was in place by the end of the 1970s, just as GPS was launched. Power inversion, published in 1979, matched GPS perfectly: the signal is below the noise, so "minimise everything" leaves the signal alone. From the 1990s CRPAs were fitted to military aircraft; from the 2000s digital space-time processing took over; since the 2010s smaller, cheaper arrays reach civil users as jamming and spoofing spread.

    25History & industry

    Emerging technologies to watch

    Three forces drive the field: jamming and spoofing have become common in several regions, new signals (L5/E5, Galileo, BeiDou, M-code, and soon LEO navigation) span more bands, and cheap multi-channel radio chips make digital arrays affordable. The result is a push toward smaller, multi-band, fully digital arrays and toward using arrays for detection and integrity, not just suppression.
    • Multi-band CRPAs: one aperture covering L1/E1 and L5/E5 (and L2), with separate weights per band.
    • Miniaturised arrays: spacing below λ/2 with decoupling networks and high-permittivity materials, for drones and munitions.
    • Beam-per-satellite digital receivers becoming standard in military user equipment.
    • Machine learning to classify interference and choose the processing mode.
    • Synthetic arrays: a single moving antenna (a phone, a vehicle) combines measurements over time like an array, mainly for spoofing detection and multipath.
    • Polarisation-sensitive arrays: dual-polarised elements add degrees of freedom against interferers that arrive close to a satellite.
    • Arrays for LEO PNT: fast-moving LEO satellites and stronger signals change the beam-tracking problem.
    • Distributed arrays: several vehicles or sites share measurements to locate jammers and spoofers.

    Who needs an adaptive antenna, and how big can it be?

    Requirements map: threat level versus room for an antenna (qualitative)
    Qualitative. Horizontal: the interference margin a platform needs (dB above what a single antenna tolerates). Vertical: the antenna diameter it can carry (log scale). The shaded bands show which array sizes fit. Hover for details.
    Each application balances threat against size, weight, power and cost. A phone will never carry a 7-element array; a warship has room but faces the strongest jammers. The cheapest effective option is usually the right one: a two-element nuller can turn a jammed delivery drone back into a working one, while a cruise missile needs every decibel a digital 7-element STAP array can give.

    Military air, sea, land

    High-power wideband jammers, several at once. 4–7 element CRPAs with STAP or beam-per-satellite, plus M-code and inertial aiding.

    Drones and robots

    Small, light, often near jammers in conflict areas or protected sites. 2–4 element arrays, miniaturised.

    Critical-infrastructure timing

    Telecom, power grid and finance clocks on fixed sites near roads where PPDs pass. Compact nulling antennas plus holdover oscillators.

    Maritime and ports

    Ships report widespread GNSS interference in several regions. Ship masts can carry larger arrays; spoofing detection matters.

    Civil aviation

    Aircraft today use certified single-element antennas; reports of jamming and spoofing near conflict zones have made controlled-pattern antennas a topic for future standards.

    Road vehicles and surveying

    Low-cost two-element units for fleets; for precise users, phase-bias-aware designs (section 18) and spoofing detection.

    27Applications

    Regulation: jammers are illegal, anti-jam antennas are controlled

    Three kinds of rules surround adaptive antennas. Spectrum law makes jamming illegal almost everywhere, yet cheap jammers are easy to buy. Aviation and maritime standards define how GNSS antennas and receivers must perform, including against interference. And because CRPAs protect weapons, the more capable ones are export-controlled, which shapes who can buy them.
    • Jamming is prohibited in most countries: in the US under the Communications Act and FCC rules (marketing, selling and using jammers), in the UK under the Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006, and under national radio laws across the EU and elsewhere.
    • Spectrum: the ITU allocates RNSS and aeronautical radionavigation bands (1164–1215, 1215–1300, 1559–1610 MHz). GNSS shares the lower bands with DME/TACAN and radars, which is why pulsed interference matters for L5/E5 and L2/E6.
    • Aviation: RTCA DO-235 assesses interference to GNSS L1; antenna performance is set by RTCA DO-228 (passive), DO-301 (active L1) and DO-373 (dual-frequency), with EUROCAE equivalents. EASA safety information bulletins (SIB 2022-02 and revisions) address GNSS jamming and spoofing.
    • Resilient PNT: the US Executive Order 13905 (2020) and the DHS Resilient PNT Conformance Framework push critical infrastructure to plan for GNSS denial.
    • Export control: the Wassenaar dual-use list (category 7, GNSS receiving equipment employing an adaptive antenna system) and national rules (US EAR and ITAR for military items) restrict CRPAs and anti-jam electronics.

    Regulatory texts are revised often; treat the specific document numbers above as pointers to check, not as the latest versions.

    28Wrap-up

    Summary: the five things to remember

    1. Direction is phase. A plane wave reaches nearby antennas with phase ; at L1, λ = 19 cm and λ/2 spacing is the norm.
    2. Weights shape the pattern. One complex weight per element: aligned phasors make beams, cancelling phasors make nulls. Two antennas give one null cone, N antennas give N − 1 nulls.
    3. The covariance matrix sees the jammers because GNSS signals are below the noise. Power inversion needs no other knowledge; MVDR adds a beam per satellite if attitude and calibration are known.
    4. Every dB of suppression counts: 20 dB shrinks the denial radius 10×. Real nulls stop at 30–50 dB because of channel mismatch, bandwidth and ADC range; STAP and calibration push the limit.
    5. Arrays do more than null: they detect and localise jammers and spoofers. The price is size, cost, export control and measurement biases that precise users must handle.
