01Introduction

What is a Local Positioning System?

GPS works because satellites with known positions broadcast timed signals and your receiver measures how far away each one is. An LPS does the same thing with transmitters you install yourself (or that someone else operates), called anchors, beacons or pseudolites: on masts, hilltops, lamp posts or towers, from a few hundred metres to hundreds of kilometres away.

A Local Positioning System (LPS) estimates the position of a mobile device (a tag, phone, robot, drone or vehicle) relative to a local network of reference nodes with surveyed positions. It is called “local” because coverage, coordinate frame and infrastructure belong to one area: a mine, a port, an airfield, a test range, a city, a coastline. This presentation covers outdoor and wide-area systems; indoor-only solutions are left out.

Typical reasons to build one:

  • No usable GNSS signal: deep open-pit mines, urban canyons, valleys, or under jamming and spoofing.
  • Better accuracy or integrity than GNSS gives there: centimetre machine control in a mine, metre-level aircraft surveillance at an airport, a drone landing on a ship.
  • Resilience: an independent backup for position and time when GNSS fails (eLoran, terrestrial beacons, LEO satellites).
  • Control: you own the anchors, so you also own the geometry, the frequency band, the update rate and the integrity monitoring.

    02Foundations

    Ranging: distance is time × the speed of light

    Radio travels at about 30 cm per nanosecond. To measure a distance to 30 cm you must timestamp the signal's arrival to 1 ns, one billionth of a second. Almost every engineering problem in positioning comes back to this: clocks, bandwidth and the path the signal really took.
    \[ r = c\,\tau, \qquad c = 299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \delta r = c\,\delta\tau \;\Rightarrow\; 1\ \text{ns} \leftrightarrow 0.30\ \text{m}, \quad 1\ \mu\text{s} \leftrightarrow 300\ \text{m} \]

    How finely can a receiver time a pulse? For a signal with RMS bandwidth \(\beta\) and signal-to-noise ratio SNR, the Cramér–Rao lower bound on the time-of-arrival error is

    \[ \sigma_\tau \;\ge\; \frac{1}{2\pi\,\beta\,\sqrt{2\,\text{SNR}}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma_r = c\,\sigma_\tau \]

    So wider bandwidth and more SNR both sharpen the range. This is why ultra-wideband (UWB, 500 MHz) and wide 5G channels reach decimetres, GPS L5 (20 MHz) beats L1 C/A (2 MHz), and narrowband systems such as Loran (20 kHz) must use carrier-cycle timing to do well.

    A pulse travelling from a beacon to a receiver
    The ruler is in nanoseconds. A timing error of a few nanoseconds, which is tiny for an ordinary clock, already moves the measured range by a metre, at any distance.
    03Foundations

    Four ways to measure where a tag is

    Every LPS uses one (or a mix) of four observables. Each gives a curve of possible positions (a line of position): a circle, a hyperbola, a straight bearing line, or a fuzzy ring. The position is where the curves meet.
    TOA / TWR: range
    \[ r_i = \lVert \mathbf a_i - \mathbf x \rVert \]
    Time of arrival. Needs synchronized clocks, or a two-way exchange that cancels the clock offset (section 05). Locus: circle (sphere in 3D).
    TDOA: range difference
    \[ \Delta r_{ij} = \lVert \mathbf a_i - \mathbf x \rVert - \lVert \mathbf a_j - \mathbf x \rVert \]
    Anchors are synchronized with each other, the tag is not. The unknown tag clock cancels in the difference. Locus: hyperbola. GNSS works the same way.
    AoA: angle
    \[ \Delta\varphi = \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta \]
    An antenna array measures the phase difference \(\Delta\varphi\) between elements spaced \(d\) apart and turns it into a bearing \(\theta\). Locus: straight line. Used by VOR, radio direction finders and 5G beam reports.
    RSSI: signal strength
    \[ P(d) = P_0 - 10\,n\log_{10}\!\frac{d}{d_0} + X_\sigma \]
    Received power falls with distance, but fading \(X_\sigma\) (4–8 dB) makes the ring wide. Range error grows with distance: \(\sigma_d/d \approx \tfrac{\ln 10}{10 n}\sigma\). Cheap, coarse (metres).
    04Foundations

    Trilateration: where the circles meet

    With perfect ranges, three circles in a plane meet at exactly one point. Real ranges have noise, so each circle becomes a ring (band) whose width is the range uncertainty. The tag is somewhere in the overlap. The overlap's size and shape depend on the angles at which the rings cross, and that is the seed of the GDOP idea.

    Each anchor \(i\) at \(\mathbf a_i=(x_i,y_i)\) gives one equation in the two unknowns \((x,y)\):

    \[ (x-x_i)^2 + (y-y_i)^2 = r_i^2, \qquad i = 1,\dots,N \]
    Subtract equation 1 from equation i: the squares cancel, leaving lines
    \[ 2(x_1-x_i)\,x + 2(y_1-y_i)\,y = r_i^2 - r_1^2 - (x_i^2+y_i^2) + (x_1^2+y_1^2) \]

    Counting unknowns. In 2D there are 2 unknowns, but two circles cross at two points, so a 3rd anchor is needed to pick the right one. In 3D, three spheres also meet at two mirror points; a 4th anchor (or a rule such as “the tag is below the ceiling”) resolves it. When the user clock is unknown (TDOA, GNSS) it is one more unknown: GNSS has 3 coordinates + 1 clock = 4 unknowns → at least 4 satellites, and the mirror solution lies far out in space, so it is simply discarded.

    05Foundations

    Two-way ranging (TWR): measuring distance without shared clocks

    Message timeline between Anchor A and Tag B
    Time runs downward. Each node timestamps with its own crystal, which ticks a few parts per million (ppm) fast or slow. In DS-TWR the second reply time is set 50 µs shorter than the first, so the exchange is deliberately asymmetric.
    If anchor and tag do not share a clock, the anchor can still measure the distance by asking and waiting for an answer: it sends a “poll”, the tag answers after a known delay, and the anchor measures the total round trip. Half of (round trip − delay) is the one-way flight time. GNSS cannot do this: satellites only transmit. Aviation's DME has done it since the 1950s.
    Single-sided TWR
    \[ \hat t_{\text{ToF}} = \frac{T_{\text{round}} - T_{\text{reply}}}{2} \]
    Clock error: A ticks at (1+eA), B at (1+eB)
    \[ \hat t_{\text{ToF}} - t_{\text{ToF}} \;\approx\; \tfrac12\,(e_A - e_B)\,T_{\text{reply}} \]
    Double-sided TWR (asymmetric form)
    \[ \hat t_{\text{ToF}} = \frac{T_{\text{round},1}\,T_{\text{round},2} - T_{\text{reply},1}\,T_{\text{reply},2}}{T_{\text{round},1} + T_{\text{round},2} + T_{\text{reply},1} + T_{\text{reply},2}} \]

    Numerical check

    Distance 15 m → true flight time 50.03 ns. Reply time 300 µs, clocks 20 ppm apart:

    \[ \tfrac12\,(20\times10^{-6})(300\times10^{-6}\,\text{s}) = 3.0\ \text{ns} \;\Rightarrow\; 3.0\ \text{ns}\times c = 0.90\ \text{m}\ \text{bias} \]

    A 90 cm bias from an ordinary 20 ppm crystal. The double-sided formula removes the first-order term and leaves an error of order \(t_{\text{ToF}}\cdot e\), here well below a millimetre. That is why UWB chips (Qorvo, NXP) used in car keys and outdoor ranging implement DS-TWR.

    Cost of two-way: 2–4 messages per anchor per fix, so channel capacity limits how many tags can update how often. TDOA (tag transmits one “blink”, synchronized anchors timestamp it) scales to thousands of tags. Downlink TDOA (anchors transmit, tag listens, exactly like GNSS) scales to unlimited tags and keeps the tag passive and private.
    06Foundations

    LPS and GNSS: the same math, different worlds

    Both systems solve the same equations: known transmitter positions plus measured ranges give the user's position. The differences are in distance, signal power, clocks, and above all geometry. Satellites are always above you; anchors can surround you.
    GNSS pseudorange (one-way, receiver clock unknown)
    \[ \rho_i = \lVert \mathbf s_i - \mathbf x \rVert + c\,\delta t_u - c\,\delta t^{s}_i + I_i + T_i + M_i + \varepsilon_i \]
    LPS two-way range (no clock term)
    \[ r_i = \lVert \mathbf a_i - \mathbf x \rVert + M_i + \varepsilon_i \]
    LPS TDOA / downlink (clock unknown → same form as GNSS)
    \[ \rho_i = \lVert \mathbf a_i - \mathbf x \rVert + c\,\delta t_u + M_i + \varepsilon_i \]

    \(\delta t_u\): user clock bias. \(\delta t^{s}_i\): satellite clock error. \(I_i, T_i\): ionosphere and troposphere delays (GNSS only, metres). \(M_i\): multipath / NLOS error (both). \(\varepsilon_i\): noise.

    07The math

    From ranges to a position: linearized least squares

    The range equations are not linear in \(x, y\). The standard trick: guess a position, predict what the ranges would be from there, compare with what you measured, and move the guess to reduce the difference. Repeat. Usually 2–4 steps are enough.
    1 · Linearize around the guess \(\mathbf x_0\) (Taylor, first order)
    \[ r_i \approx \hat r_i(\mathbf x_0) + \mathbf u_i^{\mathsf T}\,\Delta\mathbf x, \qquad \mathbf u_i = \frac{\mathbf x_0 - \mathbf a_i}{\lVert \mathbf x_0 - \mathbf a_i\rVert} \]
    2 · Stack all anchors: the geometry matrix H
    \[ \Delta\mathbf r = \mathbf H\,\Delta\mathbf x + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \mathbf H = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf u_1^{\mathsf T} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf u_N^{\mathsf T} \end{bmatrix}\ \bigl(\text{add a column of 1s if the clock is unknown}\bigr) \]
    3 · Least-squares step (normal equations)
    \[ \Delta\hat{\mathbf x} = (\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H)^{-1}\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\,\Delta\mathbf r, \qquad \mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k + \Delta\hat{\mathbf x} \]

    Each row of \(\mathbf H\) is simply the unit vector from an anchor to the tag. The matrix contains no ranges, no signal properties, nothing but directions. Keep that in mind: it is the whole story of GDOP.

    With unequal measurement quality, use weighted least squares, \(\Delta\hat{\mathbf x} = (\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf W\mathbf H)^{-1}\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf W\Delta\mathbf r\) with \(\mathbf W=\text{diag}(1/\sigma_i^2)\). In a moving system the same \(\mathbf H\) becomes the measurement matrix of a Kalman filter.

    08Geometry · GDOP

    GDOP, part 1: why geometry multiplies error

    Dilution of Precision (DOP) is a pure number that tells you how much the geometry amplifies range errors into position errors. DOP = 1 means a 10 cm range error gives about a 10 cm position error. DOP = 10 means the same 10 cm range error can become a whole metre. Geometric DOP (GDOP) is the overall version; the others (PDOP, HDOP, VDOP, TDOP) split it by direction.

    Start with the simplest case: two anchors in a plane. Each range with error ±σ confines the tag to a strip of width 2σ perpendicular to its unit vector. Two strips crossing at angle \(\theta\) overlap in a rhombus:

    \[ \text{Area} = \frac{(2\sigma)^2}{\sin\theta} \]
    With unit vectors \(\mathbf u_1, \mathbf u_2\) at angle \(\theta\)
    \[ \mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H = \mathbf u_1\mathbf u_1^{\mathsf T} + \mathbf u_2\mathbf u_2^{\mathsf T}, \quad \operatorname{tr} = 2, \quad \det = \sin^2\theta \] \[ \operatorname{tr}\bigl[(\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H)^{-1}\bigr] = \frac{\operatorname{tr}}{\det} = \frac{2}{\sin^2\theta} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \boxed{\text{HDOP} = \frac{\sqrt 2}{\lvert\sin\theta\rvert}} \]

    At \(\theta = 90°\) HDOP = 1.41, the best two anchors can do. At \(\theta = 10°\) HDOP = 8.1. At \(\theta \to 0°\) or \(180°\) the anchors are in line with the tag and HDOP → ∞: the tag can slide sideways without changing either range.

    09Geometry · GDOP

    GDOP, part 2: the covariance math and the error ellipse

    If every range has independent noise of standard deviation \(\sigma\), the least-squares position has a covariance matrix that equals \(\sigma^2\) times a matrix built only from geometry. The DOP values are square roots of the diagonal entries of that geometry matrix.
    Covariance of the least-squares estimate
    \[ \operatorname{cov}(\hat{\mathbf x}) = \sigma^2\,(\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H)^{-1} \equiv \sigma^2\,\mathbf Q, \qquad \mathbf Q = \begin{bmatrix} q_{EE} & q_{EN} & q_{EU} & q_{Et} \\ \cdot & q_{NN} & q_{NU} & q_{Nt} \\ \cdot & \cdot & q_{UU} & q_{Ut} \\ \cdot & \cdot & \cdot & q_{tt} \end{bmatrix} \]
    The DOP family
    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{GDOP} &= \sqrt{q_{EE}+q_{NN}+q_{UU}+q_{tt}} = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\mathbf Q} \\ \text{PDOP} &= \sqrt{q_{EE}+q_{NN}+q_{UU}} \qquad \text{HDOP} = \sqrt{q_{EE}+q_{NN}} \\ \text{VDOP} &= \sqrt{q_{UU}} \qquad\qquad\qquad\; \text{TDOP} = \sqrt{q_{tt}} \end{aligned} \] \[ \text{GDOP}^2 = \text{PDOP}^2 + \text{TDOP}^2, \qquad \text{PDOP}^2 = \text{HDOP}^2 + \text{VDOP}^2 \]
    The rule engineers actually use
    \[ \boxed{\;\sigma_{\text{position}} \approx \text{DOP} \times \sigma_{\text{range}}\;} \]

    The error ellipse

    DOP is a single number; the full 2×2 horizontal block of \(\mathbf Q\) describes an ellipse. Its axes are the eigenvectors, and its semi-axis lengths are \(\sigma\sqrt{\lambda_1}\) and \(\sigma\sqrt{\lambda_2}\):

    \[ \mathbf Q_{2\times2} = \mathbf V\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1 & 0\\ 0 & \lambda_2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \text{HDOP}^2 = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \] \[ \text{CEP}_{50\%} \approx 0.59\,(\sigma_x+\sigma_y), \qquad \text{2DRMS} = 2\sigma\,\text{HDOP}\ \ (\approx 95\text{–}98\%) \]

    Three facts worth remembering. (1) Adding an anchor never increases DOP, because \(\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\) only grows. (2) DOP depends on where the tag is; the same network has a DOP map. (3) DOP explodes outside the convex hull of the anchors, where all unit vectors point the same way.

    10Geometry · GDOP

    GDOP, part 3: four satellites, a tetrahedron, and the clock

    For GNSS (or any one-way system with an unknown user clock) there are 4 unknowns: east, north, up and clock bias. With exactly 4 satellites, \(\mathbf H\) is a 4×4 matrix. The tips of the four unit vectors form a tetrahedron, and GDOP is roughly inversely proportional to its volume. Spread-out satellites make a fat tetrahedron and a small GDOP.
    Row i of H, from azimuth α and elevation ε of satellite i
    \[ \mathbf h_i = \bigl[\,-\cos\varepsilon_i\sin\alpha_i,\; -\cos\varepsilon_i\cos\alpha_i,\; -\sin\varepsilon_i,\; 1\,\bigr] \]
    Volume link (N = 4)
    \[ \lvert\det\mathbf H\rvert = 6\,V_{\text{tetra}}, \qquad \text{GDOP} = \lVert\mathbf H^{-1}\rVert_F = \frac{\lVert\operatorname{adj}\mathbf H\rVert_F}{6\,V_{\text{tetra}}} \]

    12Real-world errors

    Line of sight, NLOS and multipath

    DOP assumes every range is unbiased. That holds only when the signal travels in a straight line (line of sight, LOS). If a building, a hill or a large vehicle blocks the direct path, the receiver may lock onto a reflection (non-line-of-sight, NLOS). A reflection always travels farther, so the measured range is always too long. Several paths arriving together is multipath; if they arrive too close in time the receiver cannot separate them.
    NLOS bias: always positive
    \[ b_{\text{NLOS}} = d_{\text{reflected}} - d_{\text{direct}} \;\ge\; 0 \]
    Two paths separable when the excess delay exceeds about one pulse width
    \[ \Delta d \;\gtrsim\; \frac{c}{B} \]

    Mitigations: mount beacons high (towers and hilltops see over buildings and vehicles); use more anchors than the minimum and reject outliers using residuals; detect NLOS from the channel impulse response (first-path power versus total power); use wide bandwidth; fuse with inertial sensors; and for GNSS, use dual-frequency (L5/E5a) and 3D city models.

    \[ \varepsilon_{\text{mask}} = \arctan(3) = 71.6° \]

    Only satellites above 71.6° elevation are visible across the street, while along the street the horizon stays open. The visible unit vectors all lie close to the north–south vertical plane, so the east–west column of \(\mathbf H\) is almost zero and \(q_{EE}\) becomes large: the error ellipse stretches across the street.

    How LPS and other tools help

    • Terrestrial beacons in the canyon (5G small cells, ranging beacons on lamp posts, metro-area networks such as NextNav) supply the missing horizontal directions, exactly what the sky cannot give.
    • 3D-mapping-aided GNSS (shadow matching) uses city models to predict which satellites should be blocked, and flags reflections.
    • Dual-frequency GNSS (L1+L5) narrows the correlation peak and rejects close-in multipath.
    • Inertial and wheel odometry bridge short outages; camera-based visual positioning recognises the street itself.
    14Platforms

    Ground positioning: vehicles, mines, ports and the vertical problem

    Outdoor ground networks track haul trucks in a mine, straddle carriers in a container port, aircraft tugs on an apron, trains along a line. Beacons go on masts or hilltops, usually at about the same height as each other and only a little above the vehicles. That gives good horizontal geometry but almost no vertical information when the vehicle is near beacon height. An open-pit mine is the interesting exception: vehicles work hundreds of metres below the beacons on the rim.

    A clean closed form shows why. Put \(N\) beacons evenly on a circle of radius \(R\), all a height \(\Delta h\) above the vehicle, and use two-way ranges (no clock). Then \(\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H\) is diagonal:

    \[ \mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H = \frac{N}{R^2+\Delta h^2}\begin{bmatrix} R^2/2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & R^2/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \Delta h^2 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ \boxed{\text{HDOP} = \frac{2}{\sqrt N}\sqrt{1 + \frac{\Delta h^2}{R^2}}, \qquad \text{VDOP} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sqrt{1 + \frac{R^2}{\Delta h^2}}} \]

    Numbers (open-pit mine): 4 beacons on the rim, \(R = 1\) km. A truck on the pit floor 300 m down (\(\Delta h = 300\) m): HDOP = 1.04, VDOP = 1.74. The same truck working only 20 m below the rim (a shallow pit or a bench near the top): HDOP = 1.00 but VDOP = 25.0. With 5 cm carrier-phase ranging, height error grows from 9 cm to 1.25 m.

    Practical design rules

    • For 2D tracking, constrain the height from a terrain model and solve only \(x, y\); DOP improves and the vertical problem disappears.
    • For 3D, place some beacons at different elevations (pit floor, ramps, valley bottoms, hilltops) so the vertical directions are mixed. Or add a barometer.
    • Corridors (railways, roads, tunnels, pipelines) are 1D: beacons in a line give excellent along-track accuracy but no cross-track information, which is fine for a train.
    • Keep the vehicle inside the convex hull of beacons; plan beacons at the edges of the area, not only in the middle.

    Resolution limits: how finely can a system see?

    Three words that are often mixed up. Accuracy: how close the answer is to the truth on average (bias). Precision: how much repeated answers scatter (noise, set by SNR and bandwidth). A system can be precise but not accurate (an NLOS bias), or have fine timestamps (high resolution) but poor precision (low SNR).

    18History & backup

    The ancestors: LORAN, Decca, Chayka, Omega, Alpha, and eLoran

    Long before GPS, ships and aircraft navigated with ground-based radio networks that were, in today's words, wide-area LPS using TDOA. A master station and its secondaries transmit in a fixed time or phase relationship. The receiver measures the time (or phase) difference between two stations, which puts it on one hyperbola. A second pair gives a second hyperbola; the crossing is the fix. Navigators read the hyperbolas off special printed charts (the “lattice”).
    Pulse systems (LORAN-A/C, Chayka): time difference
    \[ \text{TD} = t_S - t_M = \frac{d_S - d_M}{v} + \text{ED} \]
    Phase systems (Decca, Omega, Alpha): phase difference, ambiguous every lane
    \[ \Delta\varphi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(d_S - d_M) \pmod{2\pi}, \qquad w_{\text{lane}} = \frac{\lambda}{2\sin(\beta/2)} \]
    Same idea as DOP: the angle β the two stations subtend at the user
    \[ \sigma_{\perp} = \frac{c\,\sigma_{\text{TD}}}{2\sin(\beta/2)} \]

    ED is the master-to-secondary emission delay; \(v\) is the ground-wave speed, slightly slower than \(c\). On the baseline between stations (\(\beta = 180°\)) the hyperbolas are packed tightest and accuracy is best. On the baseline extension (\(\beta \to 0\)) they spread out and accuracy collapses, which is the 1940s version of a DOP blow-up. A phase-comparison lane is \(\lambda/2\) wide on the baseline: about 450 m for Decca, but 14.7 km for Omega at 10.2 kHz, which is why Omega needed lane counting and multiple frequencies.

    Why low frequencies?

    Signals at 10–130 kHz travel as a ground wave that follows the Earth's curvature for hundreds to thousands of kilometres, and very-low-frequency (VLF) signals are guided between the ground and the ionosphere around the whole planet. The price is a tiny bandwidth and propagation that depends on terrain conductivity: the additional secondary factor (ASF), an extra delay over land that biases Loran fixes by hundreds of metres unless it is mapped.

    eLoran: the modern revival

    Enhanced Loran keeps the 100 kHz pulse signal but changes how it is used. Every station is synchronised to UTC, so the receiver uses pseudoranges from all stations in view rather than fixed master–secondary pairs (the GNSS method, with GNSS-style DOP). A Loran data channel sends differential corrections and integrity messages, and receivers apply surveyed ASF maps. Results: 10–20 m positioning at ports and harbour approaches, and time transfer to within tens of nanoseconds of UTC.

    Why it matters again

    GNSS signals are weak and easy to jam or spoof; jamming affecting aircraft and ships has been widespread in the Baltic, Black Sea and Middle East regions. A high-power (hundreds of kW) low-frequency signal is very hard to jam over a wide area and is a dissimilar backup for both position and time (power grids, telecom, finance). South Korea runs an eLoran programme, China and Russia operate and modernise their Loran/Chayka networks, and the UK and USA have studied or trialled eLoran for national timing resilience.

    How big is a receiver? Real devices and the theoretical limit

    A receiver is mostly electronics, which now fit on a chip a few millimetres wide, plus an antenna, whose size is tied to the wavelength. At GHz frequencies (GNSS, UWB, 5G, Starlink) the wavelength is centimetres, so the whole receiver fits in a coin-sized tag. At Loran's 100 kHz the wavelength is 3 km, so the antenna is always tiny compared with λ. Physics then limits its bandwidth and efficiency, not whether it can work at all.
    Wavelength
    \[ \lambda = \frac{c}{f}: \quad 10\ \text{kHz} \to 30\ \text{km}, \quad 100\ \text{kHz} \to 3\ \text{km}, \quad 1.575\ \text{GHz} \to 19\ \text{cm}, \quad 6.5\ \text{GHz} \to 4.6\ \text{cm} \]
    Chu–Wheeler limit: an antenna inside a sphere of radius a has a minimum Q
    \[ Q_{\min} = \frac{1}{(ka)^3} + \frac{1}{ka}, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}, \qquad \frac{B}{f} \lesssim \frac{1}{Q} \]
    Small-loop radiation resistance (why tiny antennas are inefficient)
    \[ R_{\text{rad}} \approx 20\pi^2\Bigl(\frac{C}{\lambda}\Bigr)^4\ \Omega, \qquad \eta = \frac{R_{\text{rad}}}{R_{\text{rad}} + R_{\text{loss}}} \]
    But at LF, outside noise dominates: what counts is
    \[ \text{SNR} \approx \frac{S}{k\,(T_{\text{ext}} + T_{\text{rx}}/\eta)\,B} \;\approx\; \frac{S}{k\,T_{\text{ext}}B} \quad \text{while } \eta \gg \frac{T_{\text{rx}}}{T_{\text{ext}}} \]

    The theoretical receiver. At 100 kHz the atmospheric noise from lightning is many orders of magnitude above thermal noise (tens of dB at Loran frequencies). So an antenna can be very inefficient, losing most of the signal, and the SNR is unchanged because it loses the noise equally. That is why a 10–20 cm H-field loop (about 1/20 000 of a wavelength) works for eLoran. The practical floor is set by the Chu bandwidth limit (eLoran needs ~20 kHz, so the antenna is deliberately low-Q and actively buffered), by local interference from electronics nearby, and by the clock: a chip-scale atomic clock (~17 cm³) lets a receiver hold time when signals drop.

    Numerical check: a 15 cm loop at 100 kHz has \(ka = 2\pi(0.075)/3000 \approx 1.6\times10^{-4}\), so \(Q_{\min} \approx 2.6\times10^{11}\) for a perfectly efficient antenna. Any real loop has far more loss, giving low Q and wide bandwidth, at the cost of efficiency, which the atmospheric noise margin pays for.

    Passive LEO positioning: listening to Starlink, OneWeb, Iridium and Orbcomm

    Thousands of communication satellites now fly in low Earth orbit (LEO, 500–1200 km). None of them was built for navigation, but each one is a fast-moving transmitter whose orbit is roughly known. A receiver can just listen: no subscription, no user terminal, no two-way link with the operator. It measures how the received frequency shifts (Doppler) as each satellite races across the sky and, where the signal structure is known, the code timing as well. These are signals of opportunity. It is the 1960s Transit idea, revived with thousands of satellites instead of a handful.
    Doppler from satellite position s, velocity vs, receiver x (static)
    \[ f_D(t) = -\frac{f_c}{c}\,\frac{\mathbf v_s(t)\cdot\bigl(\mathbf s(t)-\mathbf x\bigr)}{\lVert \mathbf s(t)-\mathbf x\rVert} + f_{\text{clk}} \]
    Pseudorange rate: the measurement used in the solver
    \[ \dot\rho = -\frac{c}{f_c}\,f_D = \mathbf v_s\cdot\mathbf e + c\,\dot{\delta t}, \qquad \mathbf e = \frac{\mathbf s-\mathbf x}{\lVert\mathbf s-\mathbf x\rVert} \]
    Geometry row: sensitivity of range-rate to receiver position
    \[ \frac{\partial\dot\rho}{\partial\mathbf x} = -\frac{(\mathbf I - \mathbf e\mathbf e^{\mathsf T})\,\mathbf v_s}{\lVert\mathbf s-\mathbf x\rVert} \]
    One pass, straight-line model: closest approach and the slope there
    \[ f_D(t_0) = 0, \qquad \dot f_D(t_0) = -\frac{f_c}{c}\,\frac{v^2}{d_{\min}} \;\Rightarrow\; d_{\min} = -\frac{f_c\,v^2}{c\,\dot f_D(t_0)} \]

    The zero crossing tells where along the track you are (the time of closest approach). The steepness of the curve tells how far off the track you are. Only the component of satellite velocity perpendicular to the line of sight carries position information, scaled by 1/range, so close, fast LEO satellites are far better for Doppler positioning than distant, slow GNSS satellites. One pass leaves a mirror ambiguity (left or right of the ground track); a second satellite on a different track resolves it.

    Error sources

    • Orbits: public two-line elements (TLE/SGP4) are off by around a kilometre; solutions estimate orbit corrections along with position, or use operator-published ephemerides.
    • Satellite oscillators: communication satellites carry no atomic clocks; their frequency offsets drift and must be estimated or differenced with a reference receiver.
    • Unknown signals: proprietary waveforms limit you to Doppler and carrier phase unless the synchronisation sequences have been identified, as researchers have done for Starlink.
    • Beams and power: spot beams point at paying terminals, so a passive listener sees bursty, varying power.

    Who is doing it

    Iridium PNT (formerly Satelles, acquired by Iridium in 2024) broadcasts a strong timing and location signal through the Iridium constellation; it grew out of Boeing's work with Iridium on iGPS and Boeing Timing and Location in the 2000s. Dedicated LEO PNT is being built by Xona Space Systems and studied in ESA's LEO-PNT programme. University groups (notably at Ohio State / UC Irvine and UT Austin) have demonstrated passive positioning with Starlink, OneWeb, Orbcomm and Iridium signals, including aircraft navigation with LEO Doppler plus inertial sensors while GNSS was unavailable.

    What accuracy to expect

    Published passive results for a static receiver range from metres to tens of metres, depending on how many satellites and passes are used and how well orbits and satellite clocks are modelled. For moving platforms, LEO Doppler mainly bounds inertial drift during GNSS outages. Dedicated LEO PNT services aim at metre-level or better. The big advantages are geometry that changes in minutes, signals 20–30 dB stronger than GNSS (harder to jam), and many independent constellations.

    Emerging technologies and companies to watch

    Three trends dominate. Positioning becomes a native feature of every new radio (UWB, 5G, 6G). Networks start sensing objects that carry no device at all. And new alternative PNT sources (low-Earth-orbit satellites, terrestrial backups, quantum sensors) appear as GNSS jamming and spoofing grow.
    • IEEE 802.15.4ab (next-generation UWB): narrowband-assisted UWB for longer range and lower power, plus better coexistence; expected to reach products after ratification around 2026.
    • 5G-Advanced (Rel-18 and later): carrier-phase positioning aiming at centimetres, sidelink (device-to-device) positioning for vehicles, and low-power positioning for IoT.
    • 6G and ISAC (integrated sensing and communication): the network acts as a radar, locating people and objects without tags. Driven by Ericsson, Nokia, Qualcomm, Huawei and university labs.
    • LEO PNT: dedicated low-orbit navigation satellites with much stronger signals than GPS (Xona Space Systems, Iridium PNT, ESA's LEO-PNT demonstrator), and passive use of Starlink, OneWeb, Iridium and Orbcomm signals (section 21).
    • Terrestrial GNSS backup: eLoran (section 18), NextNav's metro-area 3D positioning, and national complementary-PNT programmes.
    • Quantum and map-matching navigation: magnetic-anomaly navigation with quantum magnetometers (Q-CTRL, SandboxAQ AQNav) and cold-atom inertial sensors (Infleqtion, AOSense) for jamming-proof dead reckoning.
    • Visual positioning systems: camera images matched to 3D maps (Google ARCore Geospatial API, Niantic Spatial) for street-level positioning in urban canyons; terrain-referenced navigation for aircraft.
    • Machine learning for NLOS detection, and cooperative positioning where users range to each other (vehicle platoons, drone swarms).

    Putting it together: GNSS, LPS and inertial fusion

    Driving from open road, through a jammed stretch, into a port with local beacons
    GNSS availablejammed: inertial only (drift grows)local beacons available1σ uncertainty
    No single system works everywhere. A Kalman filter keeps a running estimate and its uncertainty. Between measurements, inertial sensors predict the motion and the uncertainty grows. When a GNSS or LPS fix arrives, the filter updates and the uncertainty shrinks, by an amount that depends on the fix quality, which is exactly DOP × σ.
    Predict
    \[ \hat{\mathbf x}^- = \mathbf F\hat{\mathbf x}, \qquad \mathbf P^- = \mathbf F\mathbf P\mathbf F^{\mathsf T} + \mathbf Q_{\text{process}} \]
    Update with ranges (the same H as before)
    \[ \mathbf K = \mathbf P^-\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf H\mathbf P^-\mathbf H^{\mathsf T} + \mathbf R)^{-1}, \quad \hat{\mathbf x} = \hat{\mathbf x}^- + \mathbf K(\mathbf r - \hat{\mathbf r}), \quad \mathbf P = (\mathbf I - \mathbf K\mathbf H)\mathbf P^- \]

    With no prior (\(\mathbf P^- \to \infty\)) the update reduces to \(\mathbf P = \sigma^2(\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H)^{-1}\): the DOP covariance. The filter is DOP plus memory.

    A tightly coupled filter feeds individual ranges (even only one or two anchors, or two satellites) into the update, so it still helps where a stand-alone solution would need three or four. That is how a vehicle keeps a reasonable position through a tunnel, a jammed area or an urban canyon.

    26Wrap-up

    Summary: the five things to remember

    1. Distance is time. 1 ns ≈ 30 cm. Bandwidth and SNR set how finely you can time a signal; clocks set whether you can trust the time.
    2. Two-way ranging removes the clock; TDOA cancels the user clock using synchronised anchors; GNSS and downlink LPS solve for it as a fourth unknown.
    3. Position error ≈ DOP × range error. DOP comes only from the unit vectors in \(\mathbf H\): \(\text{DOP}=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H)^{-1}}\). Spread the anchors, keep the tag inside them, mix heights for 3D.
    4. LOS is everything. NLOS ranges are always too long. Mount beacons high, use more than the minimum, use wide bandwidth, check residuals.
    5. Pick the technology for the accuracy and resilience you need (carrier-phase pseudolites for centimetres, UWB and 5G for decimetres to metres, eLoran and LEO for jam-resistant metres to tens of metres), then design the geometry, then fuse with inertial sensors and GNSS for continuity.
